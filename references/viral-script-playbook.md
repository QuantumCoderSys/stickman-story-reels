# The viral script playbook

How to write a 60–90 second story reel that people watch to the end, comment on, and come back for. Every rule here was learned by shipping real interactive stories and reading what made viewers stay or leave.

## 1. Where viewers leave, and what stops them

Average view duration (AVD) is the average number of seconds people watch. Viewers leave at three points:

| Where they leave | Why | What the script does |
|---|---|---|
| **0–3 s** (the swipe) | Nothing is happening, or it looks like a recap or ad | Frame 1 is already moving and has a sound. The first sentence is a surprise or a consequence. No title card, no logo, no "previously on". |
| **The middle** (35–55 s) | The setup is over and the payoff feels far away | A new reveal every 8–10 s. A new voice arrives in the middle (a call, a radio, a stranger). A clock on screen keeps moving. |
| **The vote card** (last 12 s) | "It's just asking me to comment" | The vote line is short. The clue comes after it and points at something earlier, so people rewatch. |

A rewatch counts as watch time, so a reel that loops can go past 100% of its length in AVD. The clue and a matching first and last frame are what make people loop.

## 2. The 13 writing rules

These come from real feedback. Each one fixed a draft that viewers or the creator found confusing or boring.

1. **Say where, when and who in the first 5 seconds.** "You're fourteen. You're alone in a cabin. The nearest city is a hundred kilometers away." A number makes it concrete (100 km, day 9, 3:33 AM, six floors).
2. **One concrete fact per sentence**, something the viewer can see or hear right now. Use no jargon and no abstractions. "Every bite up here is dark. Except yours." is good. "Your unique immunological profile" is not.
3. **What's said matches what's on screen at that moment.** If the narrator says "the door slams", the door slams on "slams".
4. **Say the contradiction out loud.** "But Leo is at home. Asleep. Two hundred kilometers away." "But rule two says keep every light off." Viewers love catching a contradiction, so hand them one.
5. **Build up to the problem before it lands.** Introduce a person or a rule before you threaten it. Don't list problems before the goal is clear.
6. **Pass the 12-year-old test.** Every choice a character makes, and every option in the vote, must make obvious sense to a 12-year-old. If you need a sentence to explain *why* someone would do it, cut it. (A real failure: "the girl offers to tell the soldiers you're dead so they stop hunting you." Viewers asked "why would she say that?", so the whole ending was rewritten.)
7. **Characters speak for themselves, in first person, straight to "you".** Don't have the narrator summarise a character's big reveal. The character says it: "I knew it. Your blood can stop anyone turning. And the cure is you." It isn't "So that's what's in the cooler. And it's you." from the narrator.
8. **"You" never speak.** The viewer is the main character, told in the second person. You act; other people talk to you.
9. **Develop the story.** One part is a mini-story, not a single situation: arrive → get answers → it escalates → a twist → the vote. If a draft is only "a new place, then a new dilemma", it's too thin. Add answers and an escalation.
10. **Give real answers before new cliffhangers.** Each part must resolve at least one question from the last part, clearly, in the first half.
11. **Two-word votes are two physical actions** with a visible cost on both sides (see section 6).
12. **Never introduce a new rule or mechanic in the last 15 seconds.** The vote should only use things the viewer already understands.
13. **Put delivery tags at the start of a line**: `[tense]`, `[whispers]`, `[urgent]`, `[shaky]`, `[quietly]`, `[out of breath]`. The voice performs them and the captions hide them.

Write numbers as words in the spoken text ("a hundred kilometers", "three fifty-five" or "3:55") and map them to digits for the captions (`"captions": {"a hundred kilometers away.": "100 km away."}`).

## 3. The shape of one part

About 10–13 shots and 18–22 lines.

| Beat | Time | Job | Example (SUNDOWN Part 1) |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Hook** | 0–5 s | Motion and a surprise in frame 1. Where, when and who. | "Every night, something eight feet tall comes out of these woods. And it runs faster than a car." |
| **Setup** | 5–20 s | Who you are, why you're here, the person you care about | Alone in a cabin, 100 km away. Dad left for supplies six days ago and never came back. |
| **Rules / stakes** | 20–35 s | How the threat works, in simple rules | Dad's three rules painted on the wall. |
| **Escalation** | 35–55 s | Show the threat working. A near miss. A new voice. | "You only ever see them stop." One stood at the window until sunrise. |
| **Twist** | 55–70 s | Something that breaks a rule or turns an ally | Dad's voice on the radio asks you to turn the porch light on, against his own rule two. |
| **The choice** | 70–80 s | One line per option: what it gets you, then what it costs | "Turn it on, and Dad can find you. So can they." |
| **Vote + clue** | 80–90 s | The follow and vote line, then the clue | "And look at the rules again. Rule two is in different handwriting." |

## 4. Hooks that work

The first line decides everything. These worked; pick one style per part.

- **The threat, plainly:** "Every night, something eight feet tall comes out of these woods."
- **The consequence (Part 2+):** frame 1 is the result of the last vote, mid-action. "You were there? You watched them gas her, and you didn't move?"
- **Flash-forward:** "In two minutes, a helicopter lands on this roof. And your mom is on it." Then "Two minutes earlier…"
- **The impossible fact:** "Your doorbell camera shows someone at your door at 3:07 AM. It's you."
- **A sound you can see:** "Tap. Tap. Tap. Something is knocking on the windows of your train."
- **Community (Part 2+, only for returning viewers):** "Most of you chose up. So you climb." Use it sparingly; it's weaker for strangers.
- **A promise:** "By the end of this part, you'll know why they want your blood." Then actually pay it off before the vote.

Avoid starting with a name, a title card, "Welcome back", "In this episode", or the date alone with nothing else.

## 5. Retention devices

Use 3–5 per part. They are what fill the middle.

- **A HUD clock that only moves one way:** `TIME 11:59 PM → 12:00 AM`, `TO THE GROUND 6 → 0`, `TIDE 2.1 M → 0.0`, `VIEWS 2.1M → 4.4M`, `DARK CARS 1 → 7`. Viewers stay to see it hit zero.
- **Rules:** a written list the viewer reads along with. Rules create predictions, and predictions create comments.
- **Speed shown by absence:** "They move so fast, you only ever see them stop." Jump-cut between still poses, each one closer, with the same click every time.
- **A signature sound:** one sound that means the threat (the click when they stop). By Part 2 viewers flinch at it.
- **A new voice in the middle:** a phone call, a radio, an intercom, a text read aloud.
- **The person you love complicates it:** Mom calls, the little brother is in the car, Dad's voice asks you to break his own rule.
- **A real answer, then a bigger question:** "So the girl in the van was her." → "And his first post today is your sister, at 3:55."
- **The ring:** a dashed circle that lands on the clue, on the word.
- **Escalating numbers:** views climbing, the tide falling, the rows left.

## 6. The vote

- **Two physical actions**, one word each, easy to type: GO / HIDE, ON / OFF, RUN / CALL, CLIMB / STAY, LIFT / HIDE, FIX / CROSS. Avoid feelings ("TRUST / DOUBT") and avoid words you used in the last part's vote.
- **Both options cost something you can picture.** Write the choice as two lines with the same shape: *"Do X, and you get A. But B."* For example: "Run down, and you might reach Mom in time. But you'll be theirs forever." / "Cross, and you and your brother are free. But the truck leaves with Mom."
- **Balance it.** If 90% would pick one side, add a cost to it or a reason for the other. Splits around 55/45 to 70/30 drive the most comments. The clue can tilt the vote; it shouldn't decide it.
- **The vote line opens with the follow ask**, because people leave during the vote line: "Follow, and comment your vote down below. Do you run down to Mom, or cross the ladder? Run, or cross. The majority decides Part Four."
- The vote options (the `option_words`) pulse when the narrator says them a second time ("Run, or cross.").

## 7. The clue

- One plain sentence, whispered, **after** the vote line: "And look at the rules again. Rule two is in different handwriting."
- It points at **something already on screen earlier** (planted, not explained) so the viewer scrubs back. Plant it in the first third.
- It raises a question for the next part. It doesn't answer one.
- Good clue types: something that changed between two shots (Mom at the truck, then gone), something that doesn't match (the reflection shows an empty seat), something in the background (a second figure in the trees, a timestamp five minutes before the event), and handwriting, labels or numbers.
- Show it again in the vote card's second inset, ringed.

## 8. Length and pace

- **60–90 seconds per part.** Shorter is fine if the story is complete. Never pad, and never go over 90.
- House voice pace is ElevenLabs Liam at **tempo 1.15**, which is about **2.6 words per second** including pauses. **About 210–230 spoken words ≈ 85 s.**
- Gaps of 0.25 s between lines and 0.4 s between scenes, plus a 2 s hold at the end so people can read the vote.
- A shot changes or something moves at least every 3 seconds.
- `reel.py voice` prints the real length. If it's over 90 s, cut setup first, then cut adjectives. Never cut the clue.

## 9. Continuing a series (Part 2+)

1. **Record the result** (counts if you have them) in the series bible. Write **only** the winning branch.
2. **Open with the consequence** of the winning choice, mid-action, not a recap.
3. **Pay off the last clue** clearly in the first half. That's what the people who rewatched are waiting for.
4. **Give one real answer** (where someone is, who did it, why), then **escalate** into a bigger question.
5. **New vote, new clue.** Plant the clue early.
6. **The losing branch is content too.** Mention it in the caption: "A LOT of you chose DOWN 👀 Comment VARIATION and we'll release that version." That's a comment driver and a demand test. Never promise a date or a link you don't have yet.
7. Keep a **bible** per series: cast, looks, voices, rules of the world, canon so far, the season arc, and "branch starts" (one line for how each option of the current vote begins next part). See `story-engines.md`.

## 10. Pitching options

When the user gives a premise and wants to choose:

- Pitch **3 options**, each a **full mini-story** (hook → answers → escalation → twist → vote), not just "a different situation". Options that only change the setting get rejected as "not developed enough".
- Make them differ in **hook device, central reveal and vote**, not just wording.
- Give each one: a one-line logline, its retention devices, the full script table, the vote with costs, the clue, "where it goes" (one line per branch), and a strength and a weakness.
- Recommend one and say why (hook strength, clock, build cost).
- If the user said "make this" and the premise is clear, skip the options round: write one strong script and build it.

## 11. The script table

Write every script in this format before touching code. It's how you check rules 2–3 line by line.

| # | Voice | Spoken | On screen (and what moves on which word) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Narrator, tense | Every night, something eight feet tall comes out of these woods. And it runs faster than a car. | **Frame 1:** dusk at the edge of the woods; a tall black figure leans out from behind a trunk (moving at 0 s). On "faster" it streaks off screen. |
| 2 | Narrator | You're fourteen. You're alone in a cabin. The nearest city is a hundred kilometers away. | From above: one cabin in a sea of trees; the camera pulls back on "alone"; chip NEAREST CITY: 100 KM on "city". |
| … | DAD (radio) | Buddy, it's me. I'm close, but I can't find the cabin in the dark. | The radio's LED and waveform light up while he talks. |

## 12. Pre-voice checklist

Run this before spending voice credits:

- [ ] Line 1 is a surprise or consequence, and frame 1 moves at 0 s with a sound
- [ ] Where, when and who by 5 s, with a number in there
- [ ] Every line has a matching shot, and something animates on one of its words
- [ ] A new reveal about every 8–10 s, and a clock on screen
- [ ] A new voice in the middle
- [ ] Every decision passes the 12-year-old test
- [ ] Characters say their own reveals in first person; "you" never speak
- [ ] Part 2+: it opens on the consequence and pays off the last clue in the first half
- [ ] Vote: two physical one-word actions, both with a cost, roughly balanced
- [ ] The vote line starts with "Follow, and comment your vote down below."
- [ ] A whispered clue after the vote, pointing at a shot planted early
- [ ] About 210–230 words (≤ 90 s once voiced)
- [ ] No gore, no on-screen deaths; real-event stories carry a fiction note (see `sound-voice-posting.md`)

## 13. Mistakes that killed drafts

- **"Not developed enough":** options where the only thing new was the setting. Fix: answers plus escalation plus a twist in every part.
- **Confusing ending:** a choice whose logic needed explaining (faking a death over the radio). Fix: the 12-year-old test; rewrite to a choice anyone gets (run to Mom or cross the ladder).
- **Narrator steals the reveal:** "So that's what's in the cooler. And it's you." Fix: the character says it to you.
- **Recap opener:** the swipe rate jumps. Fix: open on the consequence.
- **The hook word is unclear:** "Most of you said up" was heard as "set up". Fix: pick crisp words for line 1 ("chose up") and check the voiced line.
- **Invisible monster:** a black creature on a dark forest. Fix: body against the sky, a moonlit rim, a lighter ground (see `drawing-kit.md`).
- **Over 90 s:** cut setup, not the clue.
